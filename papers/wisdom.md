## Summary

**Against a backdrop of global crisis, wisdom emerges as a vital capacity for a regenerative future.** Wisdom can be framed as a capacity for choosing what’s good and acting skilfully towards it. A core foundation is the discernment of extra-rational, unmeasurable value.

Modern systems, shaped by reductive materialism, have neglected value and dulled the inner compass that knows (and co-creates) it, with consequences all the more catastrophic given our technological power to harm what is most precious in the living world.

Closing this potentially deadly ‘wisdom gap’ depends on reintegrating intuitive capacities with other powerful ways of knowing, and empowering decisionmakers through wisdom-preserving practices.

## What's in the paper

Wisdom is a word we often invoke but less often examine across the field(s) of thought and practice dedicated to addressing the planetary (meta)crisis and a civilizational transition. Wisdom means many things to many people: from a loose sense of shrewd or intuitive knowing, to a vast array of traditions and practices designed and evolved towards living a good life.

In this first of two papers, we get to know wisdom as a practical, collective capacity, made of three interwoven strands: sensing what matters (*valueception*), understanding how things are and what would work (*sense-making*), and the *will* to act on what we discern. The paper explores:

- **Decision-making the modern way** - how modern societies came to trust only what can be measured, from GDP to education, and what that does to our shared sense of what matters.
- **Knowing beyond measurement** - why reviving our intuitive sense of value matters, with the architect Christopher Alexander as a case study.
- **Cultivating civic wisdom** - how wisdom grows through lived experience, embodiment and attunement.
- **Who's wise, and how do we know?** - how societies might empower wise decision-makers without corruption, and what that means for democracy.
